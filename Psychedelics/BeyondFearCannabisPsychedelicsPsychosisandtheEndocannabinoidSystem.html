Introduction: The Problem With Catastrophe-Centered Drug Discourse:

Discussions about cannabis and psychedelics frequently suffer from an unusual asymmetry.

The positive experiences of millions of users are treated as anecdotal, subjective, or irrelevant, while relatively rare adverse experiences are presented as though they define the substances themselves.

A person takes a psychedelic and experiences increased appreciation for life, emotional openness, a new perspective on personal problems, relief from depressive thinking, spiritual significance, creativity, or simply an unusual and enjoyable state of consciousness. These experiences are often treated as insufficient evidence because they are subjective.


Then another person develops a severe adverse reaction, and the event can become the central argument for describing the substance as inherently dangerous.

This is not a sound way to reason about risk.

The appropriate starting point is the base rate.

Millions of people have used cannabis, LSD, psilocybin, DMT-containing preparations, mescaline, and other psychoactive substances without developing permanent psychosis or schizophrenia. Most psychedelic experiences do not culminate in psychiatric catastrophe.


Most cannabis users do not develop psychotic disorders.

This does not make the adverse cases irrelevant.

It puts them in their proper statistical context.

A serious scientific discussion therefore needs to be able to say both:

Rare adverse outcomes are real and worth understanding.

and:

They are not the normal outcome of psychedelic or cannabis use.

These statements are entirely compatible.





Possibility Is Not Probability:

One of the most common forms of drug fearmongering is to demonstrate that an extreme outcome is possible and then allow the audience to infer that it is reasonably likely.

Consider:

“Cannabis can cause psychosis.”

This can be a legitimate scientific statement.

But a listener may unconsciously transform it into:

“If I use cannabis, I may become psychotic.”

Those statements are not equivalent.

The first concerns possibility and association.

The second implies a meaningful individual probability.

To establish the latter, one needs epidemiological information.

The same problem appears with psychedelics:

“A psychedelic can precipitate psychosis.”

This does not mean:

“A psychedelic experience is likely to become psychosis.”

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis examining psychedelic-induced psychosis estimated an incidence of approximately 0.002% in population studies, 0.2% in uncontrolled trials, and 0.6% in randomized controlled trials [1]. The authors emphasized that the evidence base remains limited and heterogeneous, but these estimates illustrate the enormous difference between possible and typical.

Therefore, a discussion that merely displays several dramatic psychosis cases without establishing how unusual those cases are provides a distorted picture of the phenomenon.





The Plane Analogy Is More Appropriate Than the Catastrophe Analogy:

Consider commercial aviation.

It is possible for an aircraft to crash.

It is also possible for an aircraft to experience severe mechanical failure, encounter extreme turbulence, or have an emergency landing.

None of those facts make:

“You are getting on an aircraft, so it may crash.”

a useful description of ordinary air travel.

The relevant fact is that catastrophic crashes are extraordinarily uncommon relative to the enormous number of successful flights.

The same statistical principle applies to psychoactive substances.

The correct question is not merely:

Can something terrible happen?

It is:

How often does it happen relative to ordinary use?

This is why case reports must be interpreted alongside population studies.

A collection of dramatic cases can demonstrate that an event exists while giving almost no information about how representative those cases are.





Cannabis: Real Psychiatric Risks Without a Catastrophic Baseline:

Cannabis is not risk-free.

Frequent use, particularly of high-THC preparations, is associated with increased risk of psychotic experiences and psychotic disorders. The association is sufficiently consistent that it should not be dismissed as prohibitionist mythology.

But the existence of this association does not imply that cannabis use ordinarily produces psychosis.


The enormous majority of cannabis users do not develop schizophrenia.

This distinction is particularly important because the most dramatic cannabis-psychosis narratives frequently move directly from:

cannabis can precipitate psychosis

to:

cannabis causes permanent psychosis.

Those are radically different propositions.

Cannabis can precipitate psychotic symptoms in some individuals. Cannabis-associated psychosis can sometimes precede a persistent schizophrenia-spectrum disorder. But neither fact means that permanent schizophrenia is the ordinary consequence of cannabis consumption.





Cannabis-Induced Psychosis Is Not Schizophrenia:

Psychosis is a syndrome, not a synonym for schizophrenia.

A person can experience:

cannabis→psychotic episode→recovery.

That is different from:

persistent schizophrenia-spectrum disorder.


Some people who develop cannabis-associated psychosis subsequently receive diagnoses of schizophrenia-spectrum disorders. This is clinically important.


But the statistical direction must remain intact.

The existence of:

some cannabis-associated psychoses→later schizophrenia

does not establish:

cannabis→schizophrenia in ordinary users.

It also does not establish that every person who experiences cannabis-associated psychosis was destined to develop schizophrenia.


Several possibilities remain:

  • cannabis may reveal an already developing disorder;
  • cannabis may accelerate its emergence;
  • cannabis may contribute causally to illness in a susceptible person;
  • different individuals may experience different combinations of these mechanisms.

Psychiatric causation is rarely a single-variable equation.





Vulnerability Matters, But Vulnerability Is Not Destiny:

A useful model is:

P(psychosis)=f(genetics,development,environment,stress,cannabis exposure,THC potency,frequency,…).

Cannabis can therefore be understood as one factor capable of modifying an already complicated system.

This is different from saying that cannabis inevitably causes psychosis.

A person may possess substantial latent vulnerability and never develop a persistent psychotic disorder.

Another person may possess similar vulnerability and develop one after a particular combination of stressors, developmental changes, sleep disruption, substances, and other factors.

The scientific question is therefore not whether cannabis is magically “safe” or “dangerous.”

It is how cannabis interacts with the biological variation already present between human beings.





Psychedelics Provide an Important Counterexample to Catastrophe-Centered Thinking:

The same principle applies to classical psychedelics.

Modern psychedelic research has repeatedly found that carefully administered psychedelic experiences can be psychologically intense without routinely producing psychiatric catastrophe.


A systematic review and meta-analysis of adverse events in classic psychedelics included 214 studies and 3,504 participants with analyzable adverse-event data. In contemporary research settings, serious adverse events were absent among healthy participants in the analyzed studies, while approximately 4% of participants with pre-existing neuropsychiatric disorders experienced serious adverse events. The authors nevertheless emphasized shortcomings in adverse-event monitoring and the need for continued pharmacovigilance.


Another meta-analysis of psychedelic psychotherapy found only nine serious adverse events across more than 1,000 administrations, with the authors characterizing serious adverse events as uncommon in appropriately controlled settings with suitable inclusion criteria.

These findings do not demonstrate that psychedelics are universally safe.

They demonstrate something equally important:

The catastrophic outcomes that dominate popular anti-psychedelic narratives are not representative of the ordinary controlled psychedelic experience.




Most Psychedelic Experiences Are Not Psychiatric Catastrophes:

Controlled studies have repeatedly documented substantial alterations in perception, emotion, thought, and self-experience without persistent psychiatric deterioration in the majority of participants.


For example, pooled experimental research involving healthy participants receiving psilocybin found that most participants described the experience as pleasurable, enriching, and non-threatening. Acute anxiety or dysphoria occurred in a relatively small proportion of participants at the highest doses studied, and the reported adverse reactions were managed with interpersonal support. No prolonged psychosis or persistent functional impairment occurred in that sample.


More recent safety research similarly finds that single-dose psilocybin can produce strong subjective effects while generally producing transient rather than catastrophic adverse effects in appropriately screened and monitored healthy participants.

The significance of these findings is not that psychedelics are harmless.

It is that the ordinary psychedelic experience is not adequately represented by its worst possible outcome.





The Therapeutic Dimension Should Not Be Treated as an Excuse:

There is also a second asymmetry in public discourse.

When people report psychological benefits from psychedelic experiences, these reports are sometimes dismissed as merely subjective.

But subjective psychological effects are precisely what psychiatric treatment attempts to modify.

People report experiences such as:

  • renewed appreciation for life;
  • reduced depressive rumination;
  • increased emotional openness;
  • changes in destructive patterns of thought;
  • greater motivation to address personal problems;
  • changes in existential perspective;
  • reduction of suicidal ideation;
  • increased sense of meaning;
  • improved relationship with oneself or others.

Not every report represents a demonstrated therapeutic effect.

But it would be unreasonable to treat millions of such experiences as meaningless merely because they are subjective.

The subjective domain is not a contaminant of psychiatry.

It is one of psychiatry's principal objects of study.

Clinical research exists precisely to determine which subjective changes are reproducible, durable, and therapeutically meaningful.





Psychedelics Can Produce Profoundly Positive Experiences:

A person can emerge from a psychedelic experience thinking:

“I want to live.”

or:

“My problems are difficult, but they are not the entirety of my existence.”

or:

“I have been interpreting my life through an unnecessarily narrow framework.”

These experiences are not proof that psychedelics are universally therapeutic.

They are nevertheless legitimate human phenomena.


In fact, the possibility of profound positive psychological transformation is one of the primary reasons these substances deserve scientific investigation.


It is scientifically incomplete to catalogue only cases of panic, psychosis, or hospitalization while ignoring the substantial literature investigating changes in depression, anxiety, addiction, meaning, emotional processing, and quality of life.

A 2023 systematic review of psilocybin trials in psychiatric populations, for example, found a growing but still limited randomized-trial literature investigating changes in psychiatric symptoms.

The appropriate conclusion is not:

“Psychedelics cure mental illness.”

It is:

“Psychedelic-induced changes in consciousness can sometimes be therapeutically useful, and this phenomenon deserves rigorous investigation.”




Cannabis Also Belongs in a More Nuanced Psychological Framework:

Cannabis deserves similar treatment.

Its effects are not uniformly pleasant, uniformly unpleasant, therapeutic, recreational, or pathological.

Different people can experience:

  • relaxation;
  • altered sensory appreciation;
  • reduced subjective tension;
  • increased introspection;
  • changes in associative thinking;
  • appetite stimulation;
  • analgesia;
  • altered emotional salience;
  • increased anxiety;
  • paranoia;
  • perceptual distortions;
  • cognitive impairment.

The existence of the latter effects does not invalidate the former.

Nor does the existence of beneficial effects invalidate the risks.

The important point is that the distribution of outcomes matters.

If an adverse outcome is unusual, it should be described as unusual.

If a benefit is common, it should be described as common.

If an effect is concentrated in a vulnerable subgroup, that concentration should be reported.

Scientific communication should not turn the tail of a distribution into the entire distribution.





The Endocannabinoid System Makes Cannabis Particularly Interesting:

Cannabis also provides an unusual window into human neurobiology because its primary psychoactive component interacts with a system that already exists naturally in the brain.

The endocannabinoid system contains endogenous signalling molecules such as anandamide and 2-AG and receptors including CB1 and CB2.

These systems participate in processes including:

  • synaptic plasticity;
  • stress regulation;
  • pain;
  • appetite;
  • memory;
  • sleep;
  • emotional processing;
  • reward.

Cannabis therefore does not introduce an entirely foreign signalling principle into the nervous system.

THC modifies an endogenous signalling system.

That distinction is scientifically important.





The Brain Naturally Produces Cannabinoid Signalling:

Endocannabinoids are produced naturally and often in response to neuronal activity.

One important mechanism is retrograde signalling:

neuronal activity→endocannabinoid release→CB1→modulation of neurotransmitter release.

Physical exercise can also increase endocannabinoid signalling, and this has been investigated as part of the biology underlying exercise-induced positive affect.

Thus, cannabinoid-mediated changes in mood and perception are not inherently alien to human physiology.

Cannabis is pharmacologically manipulating a system that the brain already possesses.





Natural Receptor Activation Does Not Mean “More Is Always Better”:

This does not mean that maximizing cannabinoid receptor activation is desirable.

Biological systems operate through regulation rather than simple maximization.

The distinction is:

appropriate signalling≠maximum signalling.

The same principle applies to serotonin.

5-HT₂A receptors are naturally activated by serotonin.

Classical psychedelics can activate 5-HT₂A much more strongly and in different temporal and spatial patterns.

Therefore:

natural receptor≠natural drug dose.

This is not an argument against psychedelic pharmacology.

It is an argument for understanding pharmacology more precisely.





Drug Action Can Be Viewed as Manipulation of Existing Biological Machinery:

A useful way to conceptualize psychoactive drugs is not:

“A drug puts an unnatural chemical into the brain.”

Almost every pharmacologically active substance works by interacting with biological machinery that already exists.

Instead:

drug→existing biological target→altered physiological state.

This is true of anesthetics, antidepressants, stimulants, opioids, cannabinoids, psychedelics, antipsychotics, and many other medications.

The interesting question is therefore not whether a drug is “natural” in the sense of being internally produced.

The interesting question is:

What physiological state does it produce, and can that state be useful?




This Opens a Particularly Interesting Question About Anhedonia:

If a person experiences profound anhedonia, it is reasonable to investigate whether altered reward and affective processing involve dysregulation of systems such as:

dopamine+serotonin+endocannabinoids+glutamate+opioid signalling+…

It would be premature to claim that anhedonia simply means that cannabinoid receptors or 5-HT₂A receptors are insufficiently activated.

But it is entirely reasonable to investigate whether pharmacologically manipulating these systems can restore aspects of:

  • reward;
  • motivation;
  • emotional salience;
  • cognitive flexibility;
  • affective responsiveness;
  • psychological engagement with life.

This is precisely the type of question that modern psychopharmacology should investigate rather than prohibit from investigation because the substances involved alter consciousness.





Psychonautic Exploration Is Also a Legitimate Human Activity:

Not every intentional altered state has to be medical treatment.

Humans have deliberately altered consciousness through:

  • meditation;
  • fasting;
  • sensory deprivation;
  • ritual;
  • music;
  • dance;
  • sleep deprivation;
  • breathwork;
  • entheogenic plants;
  • psychoactive substances.

The investigation of consciousness is itself a legitimate intellectual and experiential interest.

A person interested in psychonautics does not necessarily seek intoxication merely for hedonism.

They may be interested in:

perception+selfhood+emotion+memory+meaning+consciousness.

Psychedelics can therefore be regarded not only as drugs but also as experimental perturbations of consciousness.

That does not eliminate their risks.

It explains why their scientific and philosophical significance extends beyond recreation.





Reducing Stigma Is Not the Same as Denying Risk:

A mature society should be capable of saying:

Cannabis has risks.
Psychedelics have risks.
Some people are more vulnerable than others.

and simultaneously:

Most users do not experience catastrophic psychiatric outcomes.
Psychedelic experiences can be psychologically beneficial.
Cannabis can have legitimate therapeutic and recreational value.
Altered states of consciousness are worthy of scientific investigation.
Individual catastrophic cases should not be generalized into universal expectations.

There is no contradiction here.

The mistake is believing that acknowledging risk requires emphasizing it until it becomes the defining characteristic of the substance.





The Problem With “You Should Never Use It”:

The statement:

“Some people develop psychosis after using cannabis, therefore nobody should use cannabis.”

contains an implicit logical transformation:

∃x(P(x))

becomes:

∀x(P(x)).

That transformation is invalid.

The same applies to psychedelics.


The discovery that a small number of people experience serious psychiatric reactions does not logically establish that psychedelic use is inherently irrational, irresponsible, or medically indefensible.

If the goal is harm reduction, the useful question becomes:

Which circumstances produce the adverse outcomes, and how can those circumstances be identified or avoided?

That approach is considerably more informative than simply declaring the entire class of substances dangerous.





The Appropriate Scientific Position Is Not “Both Sides Are Equal”:

There is sometimes a tendency to respond to controversial subjects with:

“There are benefits and risks on both sides.”

That can itself become misleading.

Evidence does not necessarily divide neatly into two equal sides.

For psychedelics, contemporary controlled research increasingly indicates that serious adverse outcomes are uncommon in appropriately screened research populations, while therapeutic effects are sufficiently promising to justify substantial continued investigation. At the same time, the evidence base remains incomplete, adverse-event reporting is imperfect, and populations with substantial psychiatric vulnerability have often been excluded from trials.

That is not a 50/50 situation.

It is an asymmetric empirical landscape:

large population of ordinary/positive experiences≫small population of catastrophic outcomes

while the smaller population still deserves serious attention.

The size of the tail matters.

It simply should not be mistaken for the body of the distribution.





What Fearmongering Gets Wrong:

Fearmongering frequently performs three transformations.


Possibility → probability:

“It can happen.”

becomes:

“It may happen to you.”

Probability → expectation:

“There is a measurable risk.”

becomes:

“This is what drugs do.”

Exceptional outcome → defining characteristic:

“Some users develop psychosis.”

becomes:

“Psychedelics cause psychosis.”

None of these transformations is scientifically justified without additional quantitative evidence.

A responsible account should instead preserve the hierarchy:

possible≠common≠probable≠inevitable.

This distinction is essential in public communication about psychoactive substances.





A Better Philosophy of Drug Policy:

The most rational position is neither prohibitionist nor blindly celebratory.

It is pharmacologically literate liberalism.

People should be allowed to investigate consciousness, seek therapeutic effects, use psychoactive substances recreationally, and study altered states without being automatically treated as irrational or morally defective.

At the same time, society should provide accurate information about:

  • pharmacology;
  • dose;
  • potency;
  • interactions;
  • contraindications;
  • psychiatric vulnerability;
  • dependence;
  • acute adverse reactions;
  • long-term uncertainties.

Legalization and scientific openness can coexist with harm reduction.

Indeed, accurate information is more useful when people are not forced to obtain their information exclusively from either prohibitionist propaganda or underground communities.





Conclusion: Beyond the Binary:

Cannabis and psychedelics should not be understood through the binary:

safe vs.dangerous.

But neither should they be represented through the equally misleading binary:

beneficial vs.harmful.

The more important fact is statistical and biological:

The overwhelming majority of people who use these substances do not experience the catastrophic psychiatric outcomes that dominate sensationalized drug narratives.

Some people nevertheless do experience serious adverse effects.

Those cases deserve research—not sensationalism.

Likewise, the enormous number of people who report positive experiences should not automatically be treated as proof of therapeutic efficacy.

Those experiences deserve research too.


The modern psychedelic renaissance is valuable precisely because it allows these questions to be investigated empirically.


Cannabis and psychedelics interact with biological systems deeply embedded in human neurophysiology. They can modify perception, emotion, cognition, salience, reward, self-processing, and consciousness. Under some circumstances these modifications can be destabilizing. Under others they can be pleasurable, meaningful, therapeutically useful, or profoundly transformative.

The scientific task is therefore not to decide whether altered consciousness is inherently good or inherently bad.

It is to understand it.

And the social task is not to eliminate every possibility of psychological risk.

It is to replace ignorance and fear with knowledge sufficient for people to make informed decisions.

A rare catastrophe should remain a rare catastrophe. It should not be rhetorically transformed into the ordinary psychedelic experience.





Addendum: Beyond the Laboratory — Experience, Meaning, and the Right to Explore Consciousness:

The preceding discussion has focused primarily on pharmacology, epidemiology, psychology, and clinical research. Those fields are indispensable, but they do not exhaust the subject.


Psychoactive substances are unusual objects of study because their effects are not merely physiological. They alter the way a person experiences perception, emotion, memory, meaning, selfhood, time, bodily awareness, and the surrounding world. Consequently, there is another layer of the discussion that cannot be reduced to receptor binding or diagnostic categories: what people actually experience, what they believe those experiences mean, and how those experiences subsequently change their lives.

This addendum is therefore somewhat different from the main article. It should be read as a combination of phenomenological observation, social commentary, philosophical argument, and food for thought. Some claims can be supported empirically; others are interpretations that deserve investigation rather than being treated as established scientific conclusions.





People Usually Know What Kind of Experience They Are Having:

One interesting feature of psychoactive drug culture is that users themselves often develop remarkably sophisticated distinctions between substances.


Consider cocaine.

A person who uses cocaine generally does not describe cocaine as a revelation of the fundamental structure of consciousness. Cocaine may produce stimulation, confidence, pleasure, compulsive redosing, increased energy, and sometimes serious problems with dependence. Its users can enjoy it enormously while simultaneously recognizing that it is an unusually poor bargain when used excessively.

Very few people construct a religion around cocaine.

This is not because cocaine users are uniquely rational or because cocaine is incapable of producing meaningful experiences. It is because the phenomenology of cocaine is substantially different from that of classical psychedelics.

A stimulant experience and a psychedelic experience are not simply different doses of the same phenomenon.


Someone who has experienced both can usually recognize the distinction immediately. Cocaine can feel powerful, euphoric, socially disinhibiting, and intensely reinforcing. Classical psychedelics can instead produce radically altered perception, unusual associations, dissolution or modification of the ordinary sense of self, profound emotional experiences, perceived insights, mystical-type experiences, and changes in one's relationship with meaning itself.


That difference has been reflected culturally for thousands of years in the case of psychedelic plants, long before modern pharmacology existed.


Humans have repeatedly incorporated psychedelic experiences into religious, ritual, artistic, philosophical, and healing traditions. Modern psychedelic users sometimes do something similar in a secular context: they may interpret an experience as psychologically transformative, spiritually significant, or as revealing something about themselves that ordinary consciousness had concealed.


This does not prove that the metaphysical interpretation is correct.

It does demonstrate something scientifically interesting:

the subjective structure of the experience is sufficiently distinctive that humans repeatedly treat it as a special category of experience.


Modern research increasingly recognizes this phenomenon rather than dismissing it as irrelevant subjective decoration. Mystical-type experiences, emotional breakthroughs, ego dissolution, perceived insight, meaning enhancement, and changes in autobiographical interpretation are now measurable research variables.


A 2025 synthesis combining controlled and naturalistic studies found converging evidence that psychedelic experiences can increase people's reported sense of meaning in life, with these changes associated with mental wellbeing and depression measures.


This is one reason the simplistic category of “drug intoxication” can be inadequate.

The pharmacologist sees receptor activation.

The neuroscientist sees altered network dynamics.

The psychologist sees altered cognition, emotion, salience, and self-processing.

The anthropologist sees ritual and cultural meaning.

The person experiencing it may see the most profound event of their life.

These descriptions are not necessarily mutually exclusive.





The Forgotten Positive Distribution:

There is another asymmetry in public discussion that deserves attention.

When psychedelic drugs are discussed in a fear-oriented context, the most dramatic possible outcome often becomes the representative example.


Someone develops psychosis.

Someone becomes severely disorganized.

Someone has a terrifying experience.

Someone behaves dangerously.


These events deserve investigation. But their existence does not tell us how frequently they occur, nor does it describe the ordinary psychedelic experience.

The reverse problem can occur as well: enthusiastic psychedelic communities sometimes discuss extraordinary positive experiences while ignoring the difficult ones.

Neither extreme is adequate.


What is particularly interesting is that the positive experiences are not merely anecdotes anymore. There is now a growing empirical literature investigating them.

For example, a prospective longitudinal study of naturalistic psilocybin use found that substantial proportions of participants regarded their experience as among the most personally meaningful, spiritually significant, or psychologically insightful experiences of their lives.


Another large naturalistic survey found that 82.96% of 581 participants reported at least one major life change that they attributed to psychedelic use. These included changes in goals, values, spirituality, social activity, occupation, hobbies, relationships, and other domains. Importantly, 94.35% of the reported changes were rated by participants as somewhat or very positive.


A separate survey involving thousands of people reporting psychedelic-associated God-encounter experiences found persistent positive changes involving wellbeing, life satisfaction, purpose, meaning, attitudes toward life, relationships, and spirituality. A substantial majority also reported desirable changes in contemplative or spiritual practice, while 70% of the psychedelic group reported decreased fear of death.


These are not proofs that psychedelics improve everyone's life.

They are evidence that life-changing positive experiences are not marginal curiosities.

They are part of the phenomenon.


And this creates an interesting ethical problem for fear-based rhetoric.

Suppose someone says:

“Even if only a small number of people develop catastrophic psychiatric problems, one person is too many.”

At first glance, this sounds morally compelling.

But the same principle applies in the other direction.

If someone experiences a psychedelic intervention and subsequently escapes a severe depressive state, regains interest in living, reconciles with their family, abandons a destructive addiction, develops a meaningful purpose, or simply discovers a reason to continue existing, that person is also not “just a statistic.”

One person helped is one person helped.

One person harmed is one person harmed.

The moral significance of the individual does not disappear when the outcome is positive.


This does not justify calculating an imaginary ledger and declaring that psychedelics have definitively “saved more people than they have harmed.” We do not possess sufficiently complete population-level data to make such a statement responsibly, and naturalistic reports have substantial selection and attribution biases.


But it does expose a weakness in one-sided moral reasoning.

If the suffering of an exceptional adverse case is treated as decisive evidence against the entire phenomenon, while transformative positive experiences are dismissed as irrelevant anecdotes, the comparison has already been made asymmetrical.

The question should instead be:

What is the complete distribution of outcomes?

Not merely:

What is the worst thing that can happen?











The Existence of Trip Reports Is Itself Interesting:

There is a reason databases of psychedelic experiences exist.

People want to know what other minds have experienced.

Erowid, for example, has spent decades collecting first-person reports involving psychoactive substances, including categories ranging from difficult experiences and health problems to mystical experiences, perceived benefits, medical use, and other forms of subjective experience.


The scientific limitations of such reports are obvious. They are self-selected, subjective, difficult to verify, and vulnerable to memory distortion, expectation, placebo effects, cultural interpretation, and survivorship bias.

But saying this does not make them worthless.


A first-person report answers a different question from a randomized controlled trial.

A randomized trial can ask:

Does this intervention reliably change depression scores relative to a control?

A phenomenological report can ask:

What does the experience actually feel like from inside the mind?

Those are different questions.

Psychology cannot afford to completely discard the second one.


Dream reports have value.

Meditation reports have value.

Descriptions of grief have value.

Descriptions of religious experience have value.

Descriptions of hallucinations have value.

Descriptions of altered states of consciousness have value.


They are imperfect observations of subjective phenomena, but subjective phenomena are precisely what psychology is ultimately trying to understand.

Indeed, researchers have explicitly argued that first-person experience reports can provide useful information about psychoactive experiences, especially when systematically collected and categorized.

The scientific answer is not to replace controlled experiments with anecdotes.

It is to recognize that controlled experiments and phenomenological observation measure different layers of the same phenomenon.





Consciousness Is Still a Strange Object:

There is a deeper philosophical issue underneath all of this.

We still do not possess a complete theory of consciousness.

We can measure neuronal activity.

We can manipulate neurotransmitter systems.

We can map networks.

We can study attention, memory, perception, emotion, self-representation, predictive processing, metacognition, and interoception.

We can alter consciousness pharmacologically and observe what happens.

Yet there remains a substantial explanatory gap between describing these mechanisms and explaining why particular configurations of those mechanisms produce particular forms of subjective experience.

Psychedelics are therefore scientifically unusual tools.


They do not merely produce an arbitrary collection of symptoms. They can systematically perturb fundamental properties of conscious experience.

The ordinary boundaries between self and environment can change.

The perceived importance of thoughts can change.

Associative processes can change.

Time perception can change.

Emotional memories can become unusually salient.

Visual processing can become radically altered.

The ordinary narrative model of the self can become less dominant.

Meaning can become unusually intense.


These effects provide researchers with a kind of perturbation experiment on the mind.

Instead of asking only:

“What is the brain doing during ordinary consciousness?”

we can also ask:

“What happens when one of the mechanisms supporting ordinary consciousness is temporarily altered?”

This is conceptually similar to studying a machine by changing one of its operating parameters and observing which functions change with it.

The difference is that the machine in question is the human mind.





Psychonautics as Informal Cognitive Exploration:

This is where the word psychonaut becomes interesting.

Whatever one thinks about the recreational culture surrounding the term, its literal conceptual structure is straightforward:

psyche + nautes - an explorer of the mind.


A psychonautic experience is, at least in principle, an attempt to explore an internal environment rather than a geographical one.

The resulting observations may be confused, symbolic, distorted, culturally conditioned, or completely misinterpreted.

But that does not make the exploration intrinsically meaningless.


A person exploring their own mind can discover things about their emotional responses, memories, associations, fears, desires, habits of thought, perceptual assumptions, and sense of self.

Some discoveries may turn out to be accurate.

Some may be metaphors.

Some may be completely wrong.

Some may remain fundamentally uninterpretable.


The important point is that the experience itself exists as a psychological event regardless of whether the interpretation attached to it is correct.


This distinction is crucial.

A person can say:

“I encountered an entity.”

Psychology does not need to decide immediately whether the entity existed independently of the brain.


It can first ask:

“What kind of cognitive state produces an experience of encountering an entity?”

That question is scientifically legitimate.


Likewise, someone can experience an overwhelming sense of unity with reality without this proving a metaphysical theory of universal consciousness.

The experience can still tell us something about how the human brain constructs boundaries between self and world.

A subjective interpretation and a scientific interpretation can therefore coexist without being identical.





Fear of Exploration Can Become a Loss of Knowledge:

There is a legitimate reason to be cautious about psychoactive exploration.

People are not identical.

Psychological histories differ.

Brains differ.

Dose matters.

Set and setting matter.

Drug combinations matter.

Expectations matter.

Some individuals are substantially more vulnerable to particular adverse outcomes than others.

Therefore, “everyone should take psychedelics” would be as simplistic as “nobody should ever take psychedelics.”


But there is another extreme that deserves criticism:

treating exploration itself as pathological.

If society teaches people that unusual states of consciousness are inherently dangerous, meaningless, or morally suspect, it may discourage precisely the people who could contribute useful observations about them.


This is particularly unfortunate for psychology.


Many important psychological discoveries began with observations of unusual mental states.

Dreams.

Dissociation.

Hypnosis.

Meditation.

Sensory deprivation.

Neurological lesions.

Sleep.

Hallucinations.

Extreme emotional states.

Altered states of consciousness.


The unusual mind can function as a natural experiment on the ordinary mind.

From this perspective, the complete suppression of psychonautic culture would not merely remove a recreational activity. It could also remove a large informal source of phenomenological data about human cognition.

That does not mean every trip report belongs in a scientific database.

It means that the phenomenon deserves observation rather than automatic dismissal.





Autonomy Over the Mind:

This leads to the philosophical position behind most of this discussion.


We generally recognize that people possess a significant degree of autonomy over their own bodies.

People choose what to eat.

Whether to exercise.

Whether to undergo medical treatment.

Whether to practice a religion.

Whether to meditate.

Whether to sleep at unusual hours.

Whether to engage in extreme sports.

Whether to modify their appearance.


The principle is not unlimited:

  • societies place restrictions on actions that create substantial risks to other people.

But personal bodily autonomy is nevertheless a major principle of modern free societies.


The brain is not separate from the body.

If bodily autonomy has value, then some corresponding principle of cognitive and neurological autonomy deserves consideration.


This does not mean that every psychoactive substance should be regarded as harmless, neither it is in the scope of argumentation about "if every form of use should be legally unrestricted or not".

It means that the default philosophical question should not simply be:

“How can we prevent people from altering their consciousness?”

It should also be:

“Under what conditions should a competent person be allowed to explore their own consciousness?”

That is a substantially different question.


And it is particularly important because consciousness is not merely another organ function from the subjective perspective of the individual.

It is the medium through which the individual experiences existence itself.

To explore one's own consciousness is therefore, in a peculiar sense, to explore the structure of one's own experience of being alive.





A Small Manifesto for Psychonautic Freedom:

Consciousness remains one of the deepest unresolved problems in science.

The mind is not fully understood.

Subjective experience is not fully understood.

The mechanisms through which perception becomes meaning are not fully understood.

The construction of the self is not fully understood.

Therefore, unusual states of consciousness should not automatically be regarded as meaningless deviations from normality.


They are opportunities to observe the mind under unusual conditions.

Not every observation will be correct.

Not every interpretation will be true.

Not every experience will be beneficial.

Some will be frightening.

Some will be confusing.

Some will be psychologically destabilizing.

And some may become among the most meaningful experiences of a person's life.


The existence of the difficult cases should not erase the existence of the transformative ones.

The existence of the transformative cases should not erase the difficult ones.

But neither should the most frightening possibilities be allowed to define an entire domain of human experience.


A society interested in knowledge should cultivate curiosity without naivety.

A society interested in safety should pursue harm reduction rather than mythology.

A society interested in psychology should study subjective experience rather than dismiss it.


And a society interested in individual liberty should seriously consider whether competent adults ought to possess some degree of autonomy over the states of consciousness they voluntarily enter.


The psychonaut is not necessarily someone fleeing reality.

Sometimes the psychonaut is someone attempting to understand the machinery that constructs their experience of reality.


That exploration may produce errors.

It may produce revelations.

It may produce art.

It may produce philosophy.

It may produce therapeutic change.

It may produce scientific questions that did not previously exist.

And sometimes it may simply produce a strange experience that cannot yet be explained.

That, too, is information.


The history of human knowledge is full of phenomena that first existed as experiences before they became objects of systematic investigation.

Perhaps consciousness deserves the same treatment.

Explore carefully. Record honestly. Interpret cautiously. Remain curious.

And above all:

Do not confuse the fact that an experience is strange with the conclusion that it is worthless.
